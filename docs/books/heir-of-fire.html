This is book three of the Throne of Glass series. Nothing below reveals how books one or two end, but the review assumes you've read them. If you haven't, start with Throne of Glass.

Summary (spoiler-free)

The third book moves away from the glass castle and the settings of the first two books. Celaena Sardothien is in a very different place, in every sense, from where the last book left her. She faces fresh responsibilities and difficult choices, and she has to decide who she wants to be after everything she has been through.

At the same time the story widens. Instead of following one heroine, the book cuts between several storylines in different corners of the world, each with its own characters and its own problems. Among the new faces is a ruthless and fiercely determined witch with a very different kind of story to tell. Slowly, the separate threads begin to point at the same larger conflict, and the series shifts from a story about one assassin into a bigger fantasy about a whole world.

Review

Heir of Fire is where the series stops being a single-heroine adventure and becomes an ensemble epic. Giving more characters their own viewpoint chapters is the book's boldest choice and its biggest strength. The new perspectives make the world feel larger, give the politics real stakes, and set up the later books far better than a single viewpoint could. The magic and lore play a much bigger role here than before, and the emotional writing is the most confident of the three books so far. Several relationships are given room to grow, and a few of them are more moving than anything in the first two volumes.

The cost is pace. The first half is noticeably slow, with long stretches of setup and some chapters that run longer than they need to. Independent reviewers I read were split on this: one called it the best of the first three books, while another found it a step down from the quick pace of earlier volumes. Both reactions are understandable. The new storylines also do not land equally well. Some readers find one of the major new threads tedious, and switching between viewpoints can feel disjointed before the pieces connect. The heroine herself goes through a long rough stretch, and your tolerance for it will decide how much you enjoy the book. Some readers see a character finally becoming real, while others see a capable fighter turned frustrating.

Goodreads readers rate it about 4.44 out of 5 from over 1.8 million ratings, the highest of the books I have reviewed so far. I think that rating comes from the payoff more than the first half, because the book rewards patience. It is more of a transition than a complete story, but it is a transition that earns what comes next.

Reading order

The main books in the Throne of Glass series, in the order they were published, are:

  1. Throne of Glass
  2. Crown of Midnight
  3. Heir of Fire (this book)
  4. Queen of Shadows
  5. Empire of Storms
  6. Tower of Dawn
  7. Kingdom of Ash

There is also a prequel collection, The Assassin's Blade, made up of five novellas set before the first book. Even though it happens earlier in the story, the author's suggested order, which most fan guides repeat, is to read it after Crown of Midnight and before Heir of Fire. Reading it first isn't harmful, but you lose some of the mystery around the heroine, so saving it is the better choice.

New to the series? Start with Throne of Glass.

Who should read it

Readers who finished book two and want more of the world, especially people who like multiple viewpoints, fae-flavored fantasy and slow-building payoffs. If the slow, interior parts of a story tend to lose you, push through the first half before judging it.

Verdict

An ambitious and emotionally strong turning point that makes the series bigger and better, held back by a sluggish first half and uneven storylines. It is the point where many readers decide the series is worth finishing.
