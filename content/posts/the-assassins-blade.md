*A prequel collection in the Throne of Glass series. It reveals nothing about how the main books end, but the best moment to read it is after book two. See the reading-order note below.*

## Summary (spoiler-free)

Before she was a prisoner, Celaena Sardothien was the most talked-about young assassin in the kingdom. *The Assassin's Blade* collects five linked novellas that go back to that time. She works for the powerful master of the Assassins' Guild, and a run of missions sends her across islands and deserts and into the dangerous parts of her own city. Along the way she works with a fellow assassin, Sam, and begins to question the man who trained her and how much of her life he controls.

Each novella is a self-contained job with its own setting: *The Assassin and the Pirate Lord*, *The Assassin and the Healer*, *The Assassin and the Desert*, *The Assassin and the Underworld* and *The Assassin and the Empire*. Together they form one continuous arc about loyalty, ambition and the cost of being very good at something terrible.

## Review

The collection does something the first novel struggled with: it shows Celaena being an assassin. Instead of being told that she is formidable, we watch her take on contracts, plan around obstacles and make mistakes. The settings change from one story to the next, which keeps the pace brisk, and the book gives the series a wider geography than the castle-bound main books manage early on. The friendship and romance with Sam are the emotional core, and they land better here than the love interests in the first novel do, partly because they have room to develop over several stories. Readers who knew the character only from the main books will find that the novellas explain a lot about her habits and her wounds.

The format has downsides. The first two novellas are the slowest and least dramatic, and the stakes only pick up in the middle of the book. Celaena's overconfidence is a real flaw in the character, but it can make her decisions frustrating to follow, especially from someone who is meant to be so skilled. An independent reviewer made a related point: for all the talk of her reputation, we rarely see exactly how she earned it. Because the stories were first released separately, there is some repetition of setup, and the line-level prose is plain rather than distinctive. The book does build, though, and the last stretch is the most affecting part.

On Goodreads the collection averages around 4.20 out of 5 from about 1.39 million ratings, a little below *Crown of Midnight* but well above the first book. That feels about right. It is not a standalone masterpiece, but it is a rewarding companion.


## Who should read it

Readers already invested in the series, and anyone who wished the first book had shown more of the assassin it promised. Newcomers who want to start with the main story should begin with *Throne of Glass*.

## Verdict

A strong, emotionally effective prequel that fixes some of the main series' early weaknesses. A slow opening and some frustrating decisions by its lead keep it from the top mark.

**Rating: 4 out of 5**
